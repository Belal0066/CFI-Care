\section{Problem Definition \& Clinical Motivation} 

\subsection{Clinical Workflow Breakdown} The modern clinical encounter is characterized by an information paradox: clinicians have access to more data than ever before, yet possess less actionable intelligence. The workflow is currently hindered by several structural bottlenecks:

\begin{itemize} \item \textbf{High-Latency Data Access:} Clinicians spend an average of 16 minutes per patient encounter navigating Electronic Health Records (EHR). Current retrieval is pull-based, requiring the clinician to know exactly which sub-menu or tab contains a specific lab value or specialty note. \item \textbf{Multi-Modal Fragmentation:} Patient history is siloed into heterogeneous formats unstructured clinical narratives, structured longitudinal lab results, high-resolution DICOM imaging, and discrete medication lists. There is no native mechanism within standard EHRs to synthesize these modalities into a unified patient trajectory. \item \textbf{Cognitive Overload and Decision Fatigue:} The 7 ± 2'' rule of working memory is routinely violated in clinical settings. A physician reviewing a complex oncology patient may need to synthesize data from 50+ previous visits, 10+ medications, and hundreds of lab points under a 15-minute time constraint, leading to heuristic-based shortcuts and potential diagnostic anchoring. \end{itemize}

\subsection{Failure Modes of Current Systems} Existing solutions, while digitally native, often exacerbate the problems they intend to solve through rigid architectures and lack of semantic understanding:

\begin{itemize} \item \textbf{Lexical vs. Semantic Search:} Traditional EHR search is limited to exact string matching. A search for renal failure'' may miss critical notes labeled as ESRD'' or decreased GFR,'' forcing clinicians back into manual, exhaustive document review. \item \textbf{The Brittle Nature of Rule-Based CDS:} Current Clinical Decision Support (CDS) systems rely on IF-THEN logic based on discrete fields. These systems fail to capture the nuances of clinical prose and are notorious for \textit{Alert Fatigue}, where 90\% of warnings are ignored because they lack patient-specific context. \item \textbf{Temporal Blindness:} Current systems are optimized for the snapshot'' view. They excel at showing today's results but fail to highlight the \textit{velocity} of change. Detecting a slow, three-year decline in kidney function across twenty documents is a task current EHRs delegate entirely to human memory. \end{itemize}

\subsection{Safety-Critical Nature of the Problem} In the context of AI infrastructure for medicine, the cost of failure is measured in morbidity and mortality. This section frames the engineering constraints as ethical imperatives:
\begin{itemize}

    \item \textbf{The Hallucination Barrier:} While hallucinations in general AI are often harmless errors, in clinical RAG they represent major medical hazards. A system that suggests a medication the patient is allergic to because it misread a clinical note violates the core medical oath of \textit{Primum non nocere} (First, do no harm''). 

\item \textbf{Information Omission and Negative Constraints:} Missing a single mention of a contraindication hidden in a scanned PDF from three years ago is as dangerous as an active error. The system must treat missing data'' as a first-class citizen, signaling uncertainty rather than providing a smoothed, but incorrect, summary. \item \textbf{Overconfidence and Stochastic Mimicry:} LLMs often present information with a uniform tone of authority. In a safety-critical clinical environment, the system must be architected to exhibit \textit{calibrated confidence}, ensuring that speculative reasoning is clearly demarcated from evidence-grounded facts. \end{itemize}

\section{System Objectives \& Design Principles}
\label{sec:objectives}

\subsection{Functional Objectives}
\begin{itemize} 
    \item \textbf{Longitudinal State Tracking \& Trend Analysis:} Unlike standard RAG systems that retrieve static document chunks, this system constructs a temporal narrative. It is specifically designed to answer delta'' questions identifying the velocity and direction of clinical parameters over time (e.g., \textit{How has the patient's creatinine trajectory changed since the introduction of ACE inhibitors three months ago?''}).
    
    \item \textbf{Evidence-Grounded Summarization with Mandatory Citations:} The system generates concise clinical summaries where every assertion is backed by a verifiable pointer to a specific source record (Encounter ID or Lab Result). This establishes a clear chain of custody for information, allowing clinicians to instantly verify the source of any AI-generated claim.

    \item \textbf{Semantic differential Diagnosis Support:} The system aids in diagnostic reasoning by retrieving relevant patient history that matches the semantic profile of potential differentials. It aggregates disparate clues symptoms from notes, values from labs, and history from problem lists to present a unified view of evidence supporting or refuting a potential diagnosis.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Non-Functional Objectives (Architectural Principles)}
The architecture is governed by strict non-functional requirements designed to ensure safety and reliability in a high-stakes environment:

\begin{itemize} 
    \item \textbf{Faithfulness Over Fluency (The Zero-Hallucination'' Target):} The system prioritizes the accuracy of retrieval over the linguistic elegance of the generation. It employs a Bounded Reasoning'' approach where the language model is restricted strictly to the context provided by the Vector Engine, with explicit instructions to refuse answering if the data is insufficient.
    
    \item \textbf{Deterministic Uncertainty Calibration:} The system must exhibit "calibrated confidence." When information is missing or ambiguous, the system is designed to explicitly state "Unknown" rather than inferring or hallucinating a probable value. This fail-safe mechanism is critical for maintaining clinical trust.

    \item \textbf{Low-Latency Local Inference:} To ensure privacy and responsiveness, the core reasoning loop is designed to run efficiently on local infrastructure using quantized models (MedGemma 1.5 via \texttt{llama.cpp}) and vector retrieval (Qdrant), targeting sub-second retrieval latency and usable end-to-end response times.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Explicit Non-Goals}

\begin{itemize} 
    \item \textbf{Autonomous Diagnosis or Prescribing:} The system is a decision-support tool, not a decision maker. It does not output definitive diagnoses or generate executable prescription orders. Its role is solely to synthesize information to aid the human expert.
    
\end{itemize}

% --- Section 3: Related Work ---
\section{Related Work \& Gap Analysis} \label{sec:related_work}

The evolution of Clinical Decision Support (CDS) has moved from rigid, expert-defined logic to data-driven probabilistic models. However, the transition to Generative AI in medicine introduces novel challenges in verification and temporal reasoning that remain largely unaddressed.

\subsection{Traditional Clinical Decision Support Systems} Early CDS systems, utilized hard-coded heuristic rules and "if-then" logic to provide antibiotic recommendations. While foundational, these systems suffered from the \textit{knowledge acquisition bottleneck} the inability to scale as medical knowledge expanded exponentially. \begin{itemize} \item \textbf{Rule-Based Alerts:} Modern EHRs primarily use static alerts triggered by discrete data points (e.g., a high potassium value). \item \textbf{Limitations:} These systems are "text-blind," ignoring 80\% of patient data stored in unstructured clinical notes. Furthermore, they are prone to high false-positive rates, contributing to the \textit{Alert Fatigue} discussed in Section 1.2. \end{itemize}

\subsection{LLMs in Healthcare} The advent of Large Language Models (LLMs) like GPT-5 and Med-PaLM 2 demonstrated that neural networks could pass the USMLE and summarize complex notes with high fluency. \begin{itemize} \item \textbf{Clinical Fine-tuning:} Research into models like \textit{MedGemma} and \textit{BioBERT} has shown that domain-specific pre-training improves the understanding of medical terminology and clinical ontologies (SNOMED-CT, ICD-10). \item \textbf{The Verification Gap:} Despite their linguistic prowess, "vanilla" LLMs are closed systems. They lack access to real-time patient records and are prone to \textit{hallucinations} generating plausible-sounding but medically incorrect information making them unsuitable for direct clinical use without a grounding mechanism. \end{itemize}

\subsection{RAG in High-Risk Domains} Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) was introduced to ground LLM outputs in external, verifiable facts. In high-risk domains like law and medicine, RAG serves as a "fact-checker" for the generative process. \begin{itemize} \item \textbf{Grounding via Vector Search:} Current research focuses on using vector databases (e.g., Qdrant) to retrieve semantically relevant document chunks based on a query. \item \textbf{Shortcomings in Clinical RAG:} Most generic RAG implementations treat documents as a "flat" bag of words. In medicine, however, the \textit{timing} of a note is often as important as its content. Existing systems frequently fail to distinguish between a patient's "current" medication list and a "historical" one from five years ago if the semantic similarity is high. \end{itemize}

\subsection{Gap Statement} While existing research has mastered individual components rule-based logic for safety, LLMs for synthesis, and RAG for grounding there remains a critical architectural void: \begin{quote} \textit{Existing systems lack reliable, longitudinal, and evidence-grounded reasoning over heterogeneous clinical records, specifically failing to maintain temporal consistency and citation-level auditability required for high-stakes medical decision-making.''} \end{quote} This thesis proposes an architecture designed to bridge this gap by treating the patient record not as a static document store, but as a \textit{temporally-indexed, multi-modal knowledge graph}.

\section{System Architecture}
\label{sec:architecture}
\subsection{High-Level Architecture Overview}
The system implements a \textbf{Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)} pipeline orchestrated by a dynamic agent workflow. Unlike linear RAG systems, it utilizes a \textbf{LangGraph} state machine to route queries between local clinical data (Patient-Specific Grounding) and external medical knowledge (MCP/Internet tools). The core pipeline consists of:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Ingestion Layer:} FHIR-to-TOON normalization and chronological state compilation.
    \item \textbf{Understanding Layer:} Intent classification (10 distinct clinical intents) to determine the retrieval strategy.
    \item \textbf{Retrieval Layer:} Intent-based semantic search over a Qdrant vector index populated with ModernPubMedBERT embeddings.
    \item \textbf{Reasoning Layer:} A local, quantized MedGemma 1.5-4b-it model running on \texttt{llama.cpp}.
    \item \textbf{Agentic Routing:} A rule-based controller that detects when to break out of local context to fetch live guidelines via MCP.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Data Ingestion \& Normalization (TOON)}
Medical data is notoriously token-heavy due to verbose JSON structures. We introduce \textbf{TOON (Token-Oriented Object Notation)}, a custom serialization format designed to reduce context usage by 30-50\% while preserving semantic structure.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Mechanism:} The \texttt{ToonNormalizer} class flattens nested FHIR resources (e.g., \texttt{Observation}, \texttt{Condition}) into a standardized string format: \texttt{[Date] Type: Value (Ref Range)}.
    \item \textbf{Temporal State Compilation:} Rather than indexing disparate documents, the ingestion service (via \texttt{PatientState}) compiles a time-ordered sequence of events, ensuring that the "current" active diagnosis is always contextually linked to its justifying past encounters.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Retrieval Layer Design}
The system rejects the standard "fixed-size chunking" strategy in favor of \textbf{Discrete Event Retrieval}.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Intent-Driven Routing:} The \texttt{RetrievalStrategy} class explicitly maps user intent to data subsets:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \emph{Differential Diagnosis:} Retrieves \texttt{Diagnosis} nodes + \texttt{Symptom}, \texttt{Investigation}, and \texttt{Allergy} nodes.
        \item \emph{Medication Review:} Retrieves \texttt{MedicationRequest} nodes + linked \texttt{Condition} (indication) nodes.
        \item \emph{Summary:} Retrieves the full chronological timeline of all high-priority events.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Semantic Indexing:} All nodes are embedded using \textbf{ModernPubMedBERT}, a domain-specific transformer fine-tuned on biomedical literature, ensuring that "Renal Failure" and "High Creatinine" map to the same vector space.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Generation, Reasoning \& Agentic Workflow}
The core logic is governed by a \textbf{LangGraph} state machine.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Bounded Reasoning:} The \texttt{MedGemma 1.5} model is prompted with a strict "closed-world" assumption. It must answer solely based on the retrieved TOON context.
    \item \textbf{Agentic Handoff (MCP):} If the \texttt{IntentClassifier} detects a query requiring general medical knowledge (e.g., "What are the guidelines for treatment?"), the system triggers the \texttt{mcp\_search} node. This effectively separates "Patient Memory" (RAG) from "Medical Knowledge" (eg, PubMed), preventing the hallucination of non-existent details.
\end{itemize}

\section{Performance, Scalability \& Deployment}
\label{sec:performance}

\subsection{Hardware \& Infrastructure}
The system is currently deployed on a Ubuntu Server cloud instance BWS (Belal Web Services) designed to simulate a hospital's edge-compute node. The infrastructure is containerized to ensure reproducibility and ease of deployment.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Compute Node:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{CPU:} AMD Ryzen 7 3700X (8 Cores, 16 Threads). Handles the vector embeddings (ModernPubMedBERT) and API routing logic.
        \item \textbf{GPU:} NVIDIA RTX 2070 (8GB VRAM) running CUDA 13.0. This consumer-grade card was chosen to demonstrate the viability of running advanced clinical AI on cost-effective hardware, rather than relying on expensive enterprise H100 clusters.
        \item \textbf{RAM:} 16GB System RAM. for Qdrant vector index, and application services run alongside the GPU workload.
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Software Stack:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Inference Engine:} \texttt{llama.cpp} server with full CUDA offloading. The MedGemma 1.5-4b-it model is quantized to \texttt{Q6\_K} format, occupying approximately 5.4GB of VRAM, leaving headroom for context processing.
        \item \textbf{Vector Database:} Qdrant (v1.7.0) running in a lightweight Docker container. It manages the High-Dimensional Vector Space (768d) for patient encounter retrieval, utilizing \texttt{HNSW} indexing for sub-millisecond search latencies.
        \item \textbf{Clinical Data Source:} Redis Cloud connected to HAPI FHIR R5 server for standards-compliant data persistence, ensuring the system can integrate with modern EHR architectures.
        \item \textbf{Application Layer:} A FastAPI backend orchestrates the RAG pipeline, connecting the CPU-bound embedding generation with the GPU-bound inference. Streamlit serves as the clinical dashboard, while an MCP (Model Context Protocol) server allows internet connectivity.
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Latency \& Throughput}
By running specifically optimized local inference, the system achieves near real-time performance, critical for clinical adoption.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Token Generation Speed:} The RTX 2070 achieves a generation rate of approximately 40-50 tokens per second (t/s) for the 4B parameter model. This allows for a full clinical summary (approx. 200-300 words) to be generated in under 10 seconds.
    \item \textbf{Retrieval Latency:} Qdrant vector search is sub-millisecond per query. The total time for the "Query Understanding $\rightarrow$ Embedding $\rightarrow$ Search" loop is consistently under 200ms.
    \item \textbf{End-to-End Response Time:} The median total latency from user query to final rendered response is 8-12 seconds, falling well within the "usable interaction" threshold for clinical workflows.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Cost \& Scalability}
The architecture effectively shifts the cost curve of clinical AI.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Optimization:} By proving viability on consumer hardware (RTX 20xx series), the system demonstrates that hospitals can deploy on-premise AI nodes without significant infrastructure overhaul. A typical deployment node costs less than \$1,500 in hardware.

    \item \textbf{Scalability Strategy:} The system is designed as a "Pod" architecture. Each compute node can handle a specific ward or patient panel. Scaling to a full hospital simply involves adding more nodes horizontally, rather than scaling up a monolithic central mainframe.
\end{itemize}

\section{Limitations \& Open Challenges}
\label{sec:limitations}
While the RAG architecture provides a robust grounding mechanism, several frontier challenges in AI infrastructure and clinical reasoning remain. Acknowledging these is essential for maintaining engineering integrity and clinical trust.

\subsection{Technical Constraints}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{The Lost-in-the-Middle'' Phenomenon:} Despite expanding context windows, LLMs often struggle to retrieve information buried in the middle of long patient trajectories (100+ document chunks). This necessitates more advanced \textit{re-ranking} architectures, and better memory management.
    \item \textbf{Retrieval Noise and Domain Shift:} Standard embedding models may suffer from performance degradation when moving between specialties (e.g., from Oncology to Pediatrics) due to the dense use of niche acronyms and shifting semantic definitions, we mitigate this by using medical embedding models.
    \item \textbf{Temporal Reasoning Gaps:} Current RAG systems excel at "point-in-time" retrieval but still struggle with complex causal reasoning over time such as determining if a specific medication \textit{caused} a later adverse event or if it was merely correlated. We try to solve this with Agentic RAG and LLM Multi-Step Reasoning.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Clinical and Operational Hurdles}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Data Incompleteness (The Silent'' Error):} The system can only reason over what is documented. If a patient receives care at an outside facility not integrated into the local vector store, the AI may provide a confident but incomplete summary.
\end{itemize}

\begin{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Enhanced Decision Quality:} Providing semantically relevant, evidence-backed insights at the point of care reduces the risk of diagnostic anchoring and "missed cues" in complex patient histories.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Consistency of Care:} Standardizing the summarization and evidence-retrieval process ensures that every patient, regardless of the complexity of their record, receives a thorough and systematic review.
